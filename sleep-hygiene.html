RESEARCH · SLEEP & WELL-BEING

Sleep Hygiene for a Young Adult in Kashmir

A research-based guide to understanding sleep, building healthier routines and improving sleep quality in the context of life in Kashmir.

THE INNER ATLAS · Research paper

Abstract

Sleep hygiene means the habits and surroundings that make it easier to fall asleep, stay asleep, and wake up rested. This paper looks at what research says about sleep needs and sleep control in late teens and young adults, sums up which hygiene habits have the strongest support, and applies them to one profile: an 18 year old in Kashmir who goes to bed around 11:30 pm but wakes up at different times.

The main point is that the most valuable change for this person is not the bedtime, which is already reasonable, but fixing the wake time. Around that, the paper covers morning light, caffeine (including noon chai), screens, naps, food and exercise timing, the bedroom, and issues specific to the region, such as long cold winters, heating safety, power cuts, the Fajr prayer, Ramadan, and the fact that Kashmir sits well west of the meridian that sets Indian Standard Time.

It ends with a two week plan and advice on when to seek professional help.

1. Introduction

Late adolescence is one of the hardest periods for sleep. The body clock pushes bedtimes later, studies and social life pile up, and phones give you endless reasons to stay awake. Yet sleep is closely tied to memory, attention, mood, immunity, and long term heart and metabolic health.

The profile examined here is common and, in some ways, a good one: a steady bedtime of around 11:30 pm. The weak point is the changing wake time. Research on sleep regularity suggests that being inconsistent is a real problem on its own, not just the total hours. The sections below explain why, and what to do about it.

2. How sleep is regulated

Two systems mostly decide when we feel sleepy and when we feel alert (Borbély's two process model).

Process S: Sleep pressure

The need for sleep builds up the longer you stay awake and drops once you sleep. A chemical called adenosine is a major part of this. Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors, so it hides sleepiness without removing the pressure underneath.

Process C: Circadian rhythm

A roughly 24 hour internal clock in an area of the brain called the suprachiasmatic nucleus. It is set mainly by light, and also by meal timing, exercise, and social routine. Melatonin rises in the evening when it is dim and is held back by bright light.

Key idea: Good sleep happens when high sleep pressure and the night phase of the body clock line up. Irregular wake times shift the clock from day to day, so the two systems drift apart. This leads to trouble falling asleep, grogginess on waking, and sleepiness during the day.

3. Sleep in late adolescence and early adulthood

3.1 How much sleep is needed

The National Sleep Foundation's expert panel (Hirshkowitz et al., 2015) recommends 7 to 9 hours for young adults aged 18 to 25, with 6 and 10 hours listed as "may be appropriate" for some people. Teenagers aged 14 to 17 are advised to get 8 to 10 hours. At 18, the person sits right on the boundary, so aiming for roughly 8 hours is a sensible target.

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine and Sleep Research Society consensus (Watson et al., 2015) also recommends that adults regularly sleep 7 or more hours for good health.

3.2 The delayed body clock

Adolescence brings a natural delay in the body clock and a slower build up of sleep pressure (Carskadon and colleagues have documented this extensively). Many people in this age range feel sleepy later at night and wake up more easily later. This is physiology, not laziness. It means that waking very early without a plan to protect your sleep can lead to long term sleep debt, while sleeping very late on free days can push the clock even later.

3.3 Social jetlag

Roenneberg and colleagues came up with the term social jetlag for the mismatch between the body clock and social schedules. It is measured as the difference in mid sleep time between workdays and free days. Larger social jetlag is linked to worse mood, more tiredness, and poorer health. A changing wake time is basically social jetlag you give yourself.

3.4 Regularity and academic results

Phillips et al. (2017) studied college students using objective sleep tracking. They found that irregular sleep timing was linked to a later body clock and lower academic performance, even when total sleep was about the same. The practical message: when you sleep matters, not just how long.

4. What "sleep hygiene" includes, and how strong the evidence is

Sleep hygiene is not a single intervention. Irish et al. (2015) reviewed the research and concluded that the evidence is strongest for some elements (avoiding stimulants, regular schedules, the sleep environment) and weaker for others. They also found that hygiene alone is often not enough for clinical insomnia. Still, it works well as a foundation for otherwise healthy people and is the first step in any treatment.

5. Core recommendations for this profile

5.1 Anchor the wake time (highest priority)

With a bedtime near 11:30 pm, a wake time of about 7:00 to 7:30 am gives 7.5 to 8 hours in bed, which fits the recommended range for age 18. The exact time should suit your classes, prayers, and commute. What matters is that it varies by no more than about one hour, weekends included.

Practical points:

  • Choose the wake time first and let the bedtime follow. A fixed rising time is easier to stick to than a fixed bedtime, because you cannot force sleep to start.
  • If you sleep badly one night, still get up at the anchor time. Sleeping in to make up for it teaches your clock to shift and makes the next night worse. If you need to catch up, take a short early afternoon nap instead (see 5.5).
  • If you currently wake at very different times, move toward the anchor in steps of about 30 minutes every few days rather than all at once.
  • Place your alarm across the room, and use one alarm, not a chain of snoozes. Snoozing breaks up the last stretch of sleep and adds little rest.

5.2 Get bright light soon after waking

Morning light is the strongest signal for the body clock. Aim for 10 to 30 minutes of outdoor light within an hour of waking. Even on cloudy days, outdoor light is far brighter than indoor lighting. Open the curtains right away, and if possible have breakfast near a window or outside.

In Kashmir's winter, when fog and cloud are common and sunrise is late, spending time by the brightest window or outside in the midday sun partly makes up for it.

At night, do the opposite: lower the lights in the last 1 to 2 hours. Use warm, dim lamps rather than bright white overhead light.

5.3 Caffeine: set a curfew

Drake et al. (2013) found that 400 mg of caffeine taken even 6 hours before bedtime clearly reduced total sleep time. A practical rule is no caffeine after about 2:00 pm for an 11:30 pm bedtime, and less overall if you notice poor sleep.

Local note: Noon chai (sheer chai), black tea, kahwa (made with green tea), energy drinks, coffee, and cola all contain caffeine in different amounts. A cup of tea in the late afternoon or evening is a very common hidden cause of delayed sleep.

Caffeine free options for the evening include warm milk, herbal drinks, or kahwa made with only spices and almonds and not much tea.

5.4 Screens and the hour before sleep

Research suggests that light from devices before bed can delay melatonin and sleep onset (Chang et al., 2015, comparing e readers with printed books). Evening screen use is also linked to shorter, later sleep in teenagers and young adults (for example Hysing et al., 2015).

Light is only part of the story. The content keeps your brain busy, and endless scrolling pushes bedtime itself later.

What to do:

  • Stop using your phone 30 to 60 minutes before bed where possible. Charge it somewhere out of arm's reach.
  • If you have to use it, lower the brightness, turn on night or warm display mode, and avoid stressful content, work, and social media arguments.
  • Spend the last hour on calm activities: reading a paper book, light stretching, prayer or reflection, a warm shower, or planning tomorrow.

5.5 Naps

Naps can help, but they can also use up the sleep pressure you need at night.

  • Keep naps to 20 to 30 minutes, and finish before about 3:00 pm.
  • Avoid long naps (over 60 minutes) in the late afternoon or evening.
  • If you find yourself needing a long nap every day, take that as a sign that your night sleep is too short or keeps getting broken.

5.6 Food, drink, and exercise

Meals: Have a regular dinner at least 2 to 3 hours before bed. Very heavy, spicy, or fried meals late at night can cause reflux and discomfort. Being very hungry can also disturb sleep, so a light snack such as warm milk or fruit is fine.

Fluids: Cut back on large drinks in the last hour so you don't wake up to use the bathroom.

Exercise: Regular physical activity helps you sleep deeper and fall asleep faster. Moderate to hard exercise is best earlier in the day. For most people, intense exercise within 1 hour of bed is not harmful, but gentle activity is a safer choice if you notice it keeps you alert.

Alcohol, nicotine, vaping, and other substances: Nicotine is a stimulant. Alcohol and sedatives may help you fall asleep faster, but they break up sleep and make it worse. Avoid them near bedtime, and avoid sleep aids bought without medical advice.

5.7 The bedroom

A bedroom should be dark, quiet, comfortable, and slightly cool. Many sleep guides suggest about 18 to 20 °C for the best sleep, but in a Kashmiri winter that is unrealistic and unnecessary. The goal is simply to avoid getting too hot under heavy bedding, so use layers you can remove.

  • Use the bed only for sleep (and rest). Studying, scrolling, and eating in bed teach the brain to link the bed with being alert.
  • Reduce light with curtains, and cover or turn off standby lights.
  • If noise is a problem (traffic, neighbors, dogs, early calls to prayer), consider earplugs, a fan, or soft background sound.
  • Keep the clock out of view so you don't keep checking the time at night.

5.8 Worry, stress, and the racing mind

Thoughts, worries, and planning before sleep are among the most common reasons people struggle to fall asleep. Techniques with good evidence include:

  • A brief "worry list": Spend 5 to 10 minutes earlier in the evening writing down worries and the next steps for each. Harvey and others have shown that getting plans out of your head can reduce overthinking before sleep. A similar to do list for the next day was linked to faster sleep onset in a small experiment by Scullin et al. (2018).
  • Relaxation: Slow breathing (for example breathing out longer than in), relaxing your muscles one group at a time, or quiet prayer or reflection.
  • If you can't sleep after about 20 minutes: Get up, go to another dim place, do something calm, and return when you feel sleepy. This comes from stimulus control therapy, a core part of CBT-I.

6. Kashmir specific considerations

6.1 Seasons and daylight

Kashmir has sharp seasonal changes. In summer, dawn comes very early and days are long. In winter, sunrise is late and evenings get dark and cold early, which encourages staying indoors under blankets.

Low winter light matters because it can weaken the body clock's anchor and may add to low mood and sluggishness for some people. Practical steps: take a short daytime walk, sit by the sunniest window, and keep the same wake time even when it is dark outside.

6.2 Where Kashmir sits relative to IST

Indian Standard Time is based on a meridian at about 82.5° E. Srinagar is at roughly 74.8° E, so local solar time is behind IST by around half an hour compared with the reference meridian.

In practice, sunrise and sunset happen later on the clock than in eastern India. This is a plausible reason why evenings stay light later in summer, though the effect has not been studied specifically for Kashmir. It reinforces the value of using real daylight, not the clock, to anchor your day.

6.3 Heating and safety

This is the most important safety point in the paper. Traditional winter heating such as the bukhari (wood or fuel stove), the kangri (a pot of embers), charcoal burners, and gas or fuel heaters can produce carbon monoxide, which has no smell and can be fatal during sleep.
  • Never sleep in a closed room with a burning charcoal, wood, or fuel heater, and never take a kangri or live embers to bed in a room without fresh air.
  • Make sure chimneys and flues are clear and the room is ventilated.
  • If possible, use a CO alarm, and consider electric blankets (used as instructed), hot water bottles, and warm layers instead of open flames.
  • Headache, dizziness, nausea, or confusion in a heated, closed room is an emergency: get fresh air and seek medical help immediately.

6.4 Power cuts and connectivity

Irregular electricity can interrupt sleep through sudden darkness, loss of heating, and alarm failures. Keep a battery powered or phone based backup alarm and a small torch. Internet disruptions, when they happen, can suddenly change evening habits, so having an offline wind down routine (a book, journaling, prayer) helps.

6.5 Fajr, Ramadan, and religious routines

For many people in Kashmir, waking for Fajr is a meaningful part of the day, and it can fit well with a regular schedule. There are two approaches:

If you wake for Fajr daily: Treat that as your anchor and move your bedtime earlier so total sleep stays near 7 to 8 hours (for example, around 10:00 to 10:30 pm for a pre dawn wake in winter). A short nap later in the morning is fine.

If you pray Fajr and go back to sleep: Keep the second wake time fixed rather than changing it.

During Ramadan: Schedules change with suhoor and iftar, and sleep is commonly split. Keep one consistent main sleep block, use a short afternoon nap, avoid heavy late meals and caffeine at suhoor, get morning light, and return to your regular schedule soon after Ramadan.

6.6 Altitude, cold, and dryness

Srinagar is at roughly 1,600 m, which is not high enough to cause altitude related sleep problems in most people, but indoor heating can dry the air. Dry air can irritate the nose and throat and disturb sleep.

A bowl of water in the room or a humidifier can help if you notice dryness. Dress warmly in layers rather than overheating the room.

7. Special issues relevant at age 18

7.1 The pull of late nights

The habits of late adolescence (study sessions, gaming, social media, group chats) are the main reason bedtimes slide later. Your current bedtime is decent, so the aim is to protect it. A useful rule: decide in advance what ends the evening (for example, a set time when the phone goes on charge), instead of relying on willpower in the moment.

7.2 Exams and all nighters

Giving up sleep to study usually backfires. Sleep after learning helps lock in memory, and lack of sleep hurts attention and recall. Study earlier in the day if possible, use short blocks, and avoid caffeine after midday during exam periods.

7.3 Sleep extension and weekends

Mah et al. (2011) found that athletes who extended their sleep to about 10 hours improved their performance and mood, showing that many young people are mildly sleep deprived. If you feel tired during the day, adding 30 to 60 minutes of consistent sleep time (moving bedtime slightly earlier while keeping the same wake time) is better than sleeping in on weekends.

8. Common problems and what to try

9. A two week implementation plan

The following plan introduces changes gradually rather than trying to change every habit at once.

  1. Days 1 to 3: Measure Keep a simple sleep diary (see Section 10). Don't change anything yet, just note your actual bedtime, how long it took to fall asleep, wake time, caffeine, naps, and screen use.
  2. Days 4 to 7: Anchor Pick a wake time between 7:00 and 7:30 (or earlier if Fajr is your anchor). Get up at that time every day, even after a bad night. Get 10 to 20 minutes of morning light. Set a caffeine cutoff of 2:00 pm.
  3. Days 8 to 10: Protect the evening Add a phone away time 30 to 60 minutes before bed. Dim the lights. Add a 5 minute worry list earlier in the evening.
  4. Days 11 to 14: Fine tune Adjust naps, evening meal timing, and room setup. If you still feel tired during the day, move bedtime 15 to 30 minutes earlier.

After two weeks, review your diary. Judge success by consistency, daytime alertness, and mood, not by one perfect night. Sleep improves gradually, and some nights will still be poor.

10. When to seek professional help

Consider speaking with a doctor or sleep specialist if:

  • You have trouble sleeping at least three nights per week for over three months, even with good habits (possible insomnia disorder).
  • You snore loudly, gasp or stop breathing in your sleep, or wake up tired despite enough hours (possible sleep apnea).
  • You have a strong urge to move your legs at night, or sudden sleep attacks during the day.
  • You sleep very late and cannot shift earlier despite sustained effort (possible delayed sleep wake phase disorder).
  • Low mood, anxiety, or hopelessness comes along with the sleep problems.
  • You rely on alcohol, sedatives, or other substances to sleep.

The first choice treatment for chronic insomnia is cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), which has stronger long term results than sleeping pills (Qaseem et al., 2016, American College of Physicians). Sleeping tablets should only be used under medical guidance.

11. Limitations

Much sleep research is done in Western populations, and little work is specific to Kashmir. The regional sections combine general science with local context and should be read as reasoned application, not as findings from Kashmir studies.

Many studies are observational, so they show associations rather than proof of cause.

This paper is based only on the information provided (age, bedtime, changing wake time) and does not assess health conditions, medications, or workload.

12. Conclusion

For an 18 year old in Kashmir who already goes to bed at a reasonable time, the best improvement is simple: choose a consistent wake time, get morning light, cut caffeine after early afternoon, and protect a calm wind down. Adapt this to Kashmir's seasons, treat heating safety as non negotiable, and handle prayer and Ramadan schedules with one fixed main sleep block.

Educational disclaimer

This paper is for learning only. It is not medical advice and does not replace a check up with a doctor. If you experience persistent sleep difficulties or concerning symptoms, consult a qualified healthcare professional.
